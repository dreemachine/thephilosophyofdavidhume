Perception

Perceptual Architecture

  • Perceptions divide into impressions (original/sensation and secondary/reflection) and ideas (memory and imagination)
  • Passions are secondary impressions (impressions of reflection) - they are caused by other perceptions
  • All passions are simple feelings, not complex - they cannot be decomposed into further parts

The Copy Principle (see node)

Fundamental Characteristics of Ideas/Impressions:

  • Distinction in kind: Exclusive and exhaustive - every perception is either an impression OR an idea, never both
  • Distinction in Degree: Force and liveliness (impressions have high degrees, ideas have low degrees)
  • Force: Relational quality - how perceptions interact and compete for attention in the mind; about making a difference, having power
  • Liveliness: Intrinsic quality - belongs to the perception itself, independent of other mental contents; a feature you can inspect in the perception itself

Examples:

  • Impressions: Direct sensory experience (seeing someone in front of you), emotions/passions
  • Ideas: Memories, imaginings, abstract thoughts

Concerns:

  • The boundary problem: Hume admits problematic cases where "they may very nearly approach to each other" - in sleep, fever, madness, violent emotions, or when impressions are "faint and low." This suggests a spectrum rather than discrete categories.

necessary connexion

Psychological compulsion vs Logical compulsion

"After a frequent repetition, I find, that upon the appearance of one of the objects, the mind is determined by custom to consider its usual attendant... 'Tis this impression, then, or determination, which affords me the idea of necessity."

Necessary Connexion: the feeling of being determined by habit/custom to move from one idea to another. It's not in the objects themselves but in the mind's sense of compulsion. This makes it a unique category - a non-passionate secondary impression that grounds our concept of causal necessity. Where our idea of causal necessity comes from - not from the objects themselves, but from the mind's habit-formed feeling of being determined to move from cause to effect.

  • Empirically, all we ever observe is regularity: rocks hitting windows, windows shattering, this happening repeatedly
  • Mentally, we develop the habit of expecting shattered windows when we see flying rocks
  • What we DON'T observe is some mysterious "causal power" or "necessary force" flowing from rock to window

The mind creates the association regardless of whether there's some deeper metaphysical "causal process." Even if there were no actual causal powers in nature, we'd still form these mental habits based on observed regularities; ie the classic example of "the sun will rise tomorrow" being an inference rather then a deductive truth.

Counter-examples

Challenges to the force/liveliness distinction:

  1. Eureka moments - sudden insights that seem more forceful than many impressions
  2. Vivid memories - remembering embarrassment often feels stronger than the original experience
  3. Anxiety scenarios - imagining worst-case outcomes can dominate actual present experience

Hume's Solution:

  • What's actually forceful in these cases are secondary impressions  (emotional responses) triggered by the ideas
  • The memory itself isn't forceful - the shame/pain it causes is the forceful element
  • This preserves Hume's framework: impressions remain supreme in force and liveliness
Copy Principle

The Copy Principle

Core claim: Simple ideas are exact copies of simple impressions and cannot be obtained any other way.

Implications: Severe constraints on imagination - you cannot conceive simple ideas for which you lack corresponding simple impressions.

Hume's Example for the copy principle: To give a child an idea of scarlet or orange, of sweet or bitter, you present the objects (impressions), not try to produce impressions from ideas.

The Missing Shade of Blue

The Missing Shade of Blue Challenge

The problem: Hume claims someone who has seen all shades of blue except one particular shade could still form an idea of that missing shade by placing it in the spectrum. This appears to violate the copy principle since it's a simple idea without a prior simple impression.

Solution A

Solution 1: Complex Idea Strategy (the office hours idea where i learn i'm very silly for thinking colors could be simple)

Argument: The missing shade isn't actually simple but complex, built from:

  • Hue, saturation, and value components from other blue impressions
  • Associative principles combining these elements

Advantages: Preserves copy principle for simple ideas

Costs:

  • Makes most colors complex rather than simple
  • May not align with how Hume presents the example
  • Requires technical color theory distinctions
Solution B

Solution 2: Martin's Conceptual Interpretation

Key distinction: Separates causal from conceptual dependence

  • Impressions: Presentations (direct presence of objects)
  • Ideas: Representations (re-presentations)

Core claim: Representation is conceptually dependent on presentation, not causally/temporally dependent.

Implication: You need the general capacity for visual presentation to have visual representations, but not the specific impression of each particular object.

Advantages: Provides stronger distinction in kind, maintains copy principle structure

Costs:

  • Too permissive - seems to allow imagining things Hume explicitly says we cannot (pineapple taste, unfelt emotions)
  • Conflicts with other textual evidence about imagination's limits
